<p>What do vampires, The Matrix, and megalophobia have in common? They’re all discussed in this episode about the ways artists and scientists interpret and convey important messages. Whether it’s for building maps, or creating color palettes, guests Julia and Jake agree that physical experiences help to better understand our environments.</p>


<div class="ep-row">
  <div class="ep-column">
    <figure>
      <img src="/images/JuliaOldham.png" alt="Julia Oldham">
      <figcaption><b><a href = "https://www.juliaoldham.com/">Julia Oldham</a></b>is a digital media artist with a research intensive practice, frequently involving scientists as collaborators. As a <a href = "https://fluxnetart.github.io/Julia/">FLUXNET artist</a> in residence she is collaborating with Chris Still at Oregon State University. She is creating a multi-channel video installation that explores ecological cycles through color data using a range of lens-based technologies. By transforming scientific imagery and field data into immersive experiences, Julia’s artwork offers a dynamic and expansive visual examination of changing landscapes in the Pacific Northwest USA.</figcaption>
    </figure>

  <div class="ep-column">
    <figure>
      <img src="/images/JakeNelson.png" alt="Jake Nelson">
      <figcaption><b><a href="https://www.polarimpactnetwork.org/features/jacqueline-hung">Jake Nelson</a></b> is a researcher at the Max Planck Institute for Biogeochemistry who studies how plants use water and how carbon and water cycles interact across ecosystems. Using machine learning and physiological insights, he estimates plant transpiration and ecosystem fluxes. As part of the <a href = "https://www.bgc-jena.mpg.de/en/news-fluxcom-x?c=4844260">FLUXCOM</a> team, Jake helps produce global, data-driven estimates of terrestrial carbon, energy, and water fluxes, advancing our understanding of ecosystem responses to environmental change.
</figcaption>
